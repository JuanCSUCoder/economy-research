# Colombia's public finances, 2022-2026, and the “Ley de Rescate Económico”

**Cut-off date:** 2026-11-03<br>
**Coverage:** Central National Government (GNC)<br>
**Status of the bill:** filed on 2026-10-29; **not law** (first debate pending)<br>
**Full report (Spanish):** `2026-11-03-finanzas-publicas-colombia-ley-rescate.es.md`

## Summary

- **The fiscal deterioration is real and structural.** The central government deficit
  moved from 5.3% of GDP in 2022 to 6.7% in 2024, 6.4% in 2025 and an official 7.2%
  projection for 2026 (7.8% according to the CARF). For 2027, without measures, the
  government projects 9.4% of GDP (9.5% CARF), the highest in recent history. Net debt
  is about 60% of GDP and would reach 66.2%-67.4% in 2027, close to the 71% fiscal-rule
  ceiling (MHCP, 2026a; CARF, 2026a).

- **The 2023 consolidation was cyclical, not structural.** The deficit fell to 4.2% of GDP
  and net debt to 53.4% on the back of exceptional revenue (high oil prices, inflation and
  the 2022 tax reform). Once revenue normalised, the deficit returned to 6.7% in 2024.
  The primary balance stayed negative every year: -1.0% (2022), -0.3% (2023), -2.4%
  (2024), -3.5% (2025) and -3.3% (2026) (MHCP, 2024; CARF, 2026a).

- **Interest and rigid spending are the binding constraints.** Interest rose from 3.9% of
  GDP in 2023 to 4.4% in 2024 and is projected at 4.9% in 2027 (COP 104.7 trillion),
  about one third of tax revenue. The 2027 budget allocates COP 147.1 trillion to debt
  service (23.2% of the total), more than the COP 94.6 trillion for public investment (MHCP, 2026a; Congreso de la República, 2026).

- **The “Ley de Rescate” is quantitatively relevant but back-loaded.** It targets a
  2.2%-2.4% of GDP adjustment in 2027 and 4.1%-4.7% by 2030. In 2027, however, 87% of
  the effect comes from spending cuts (2.04% of GDP) and only 0.32% from revenue; the
  new VAT, personal income tax and health contributions start in 2028. The critical year
  depends almost entirely on the hardest part to execute (MHCP, 2026b).

- **It is a tax shift, not only an adjustment.** The bill cuts the corporate rate from 35%
  to 33% (2027) and 31% (2028), softens the wealth tax (threshold 95,500 UVT, rates
  0.5%/1.0%, until 2030) and broadens VAT to everyday goods (coffee, sugar, pasta,
  chocolate, fruit and vegetables, internet for low-income households, low-end computers
  and phones) from 2028. EfectIVA, a cash VAT refund for about 35 million people, is
  meant to offset the impact. Progressivity is conditional on that refund working (MHCP, 2026b; La República, 2026c).

- **The package stabilises debt; it does not reduce it.** The official scenario with the
  bill keeps net debt around 64.2% of GDP through 2030, above the 55% anchor and seven
  points below the 71% ceiling. The government itself concedes the package is “barely
  enough” to stabilise debt. It is a first step of a multi-year consolidation, not a rescue
  that restores prudent debt levels (MHCP, 2026b; CARF, 2026a).

- **The main risk is political and credibility-related.** The government holds 63 of 90
  seats in the economic committees (70%), but not committed votes: the Centro
  Democrático and parts of the coalition reject the VAT and health-contribution increases.
  The legislative term ends on 2026-12-16 and four debates are required. If the tax chapter
  is diluted and spending execution falls short, the 2027 deficit would remain near 8%-9%
  and a market-driven, more regressive adjustment would follow (Orza, 2026; El Espectador, 2026a).

- **Unfunded liabilities sit outside the bill.** The 2026-08-10 earthquake caused
  COP 30 trillion in direct damage, later revised to COP 54 trillion in reconstruction
  costs (2.6% of GDP), and triggered an emergency decree still under Constitutional
  Court review. Health-sector arrears are about COP 27.8 trillion (60.5% overdue), with
  an actuarial UPC shortfall estimated at COP 33.4 trillion. These compete for the same
  fiscal space (Presidencia de la República de Colombia, 2026; Infobae, 2026; Portafolio, 2025).

- **Bottom line.** The diagnosis is broadly right and the direction of the bill is
  reasonable, but the programme is back-loaded, distributionally conditional and
  politically fragile. A more credible path would front-load feasible spending measures,
  phase the tax changes realistically, commit to verifiable EfectIVA coverage, and set a
  multi-year primary-balance and debt path with independent monitoring. Without that,
  Colombia risks a forced adjustment that is larger and more regressive than the one now
  on the table.
